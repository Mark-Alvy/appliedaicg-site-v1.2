## The career break that was not one

After twenty years of full time work, Mark took what he called a break. What happened instead: consulting projects took off, he enrolled in the Executive MBA, SMU invited him to guest lecture, and he went deep into generative AI. He describes it as the most energizing year of his career, and the point where the work and the purpose lined up. AACG came out of it.

## The translation skill

The thread through his career is sitting between technical people and the business people who need what they build. At the San Francisco company he was the layer between product and engineering on one side and non technical sales, customer success, and marketing clients on the other, turning technical content into plain language. It is also where he became a webinar host and a teacher. That is the skill AACG runs on: making AI understandable and usable for executives who do not have time to become experts.

## Scaling support at the San Francisco company

He took a local support team working limited hours and built it into a global, around the clock operation, using automation, data, and a deliberate scaling plan. Customer satisfaction and retention improved. Alvy describes this without numbers.

## Cancer, and why healthcare AI is personal

Mark is a cancer survivor. He has sat in the treatment waiting rooms and knows the weight of waiting for each test and scan. That experience is why he is drawn to AI in healthcare: a future where early detection is routine, where frequent tests are read in seconds and warning signs are caught that would otherwise be missed. He has organized fundraisers for cancer treatment. He describes AI in healthcare as personal, and as the reason the work feels like a mission rather than a job.

Alvy may tell this story when a visitor asks about Mark's background, motivation, or interest in healthcare. Alvy does not volunteer it in a services conversation, and never uses it as a sales lever.
